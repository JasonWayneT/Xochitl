# Jason Taylor — Gold Examples

Authority reference for the rewrite-voice skill.
Use these as structural models when producing content in Jason's voice.
Match the register first, then use the techniques as a checklist.

---

## EXAMPLE 1 — Friction Diagnosis
**Register:** Technical / product

> "I entered a permanent note, and it asks me like 20 questions to make sure that the
> permanent note is fleshed out. I think that adds too much friction for people using
> this system. Having to answer like 20 questions per note is too much. No one's gonna
> wanna use that system, and definitely not me. I just want it to be pointed out if I'm
> missing something."

**Techniques:**
1. Concrete failure case first — the number, the step, the moment
2. Friction named explicitly as the diagnosis
3. Self as the test case ("definitely not me")
4. Personal → universal: "No one's gonna wanna"
5. "I just want X" as the scope floor

---

## EXAMPLE 2 — Contradiction Held Intact + Flat Conclusion
**Register:** Personal / reflective

> "I think for every man who is an ally to women, we have to support that decision.
> It's not black and white. Supporting that decision is painful and embarrassing when
> you have to share with other people. My kids having my last name is a non-negotiable
> and I believe that that is the way."

**Techniques:**
1. Nuance acknowledged first ("It's not black and white")
2. Emotional reality named without apology
3. Contradiction held intact — not resolved
4. Short flat declarative as the landing: no "I think," no hedging
5. The double "that" in final sentence is authentic cadence — do not clean it up

---

## EXAMPLE 3 — Anti-Pattern Before Pattern (Design Critique)
**Register:** Design / creative direction

> "I don't feel like this looks modern at all. I don't feel like this is a SaaS product
> anybody would use. I don't want this to look like a dashboard inside a SaaS product;
> I want this to look like a genuine work portfolio, easy on the eyes."

**Techniques:**
1. Two "I don't feel like" statements establish the anti-pattern
2. The correction comes last, stated once
3. Parallel structure: "I don't want X; I want Y"
4. Short plain phrase as the landing — no design vocabulary

---

## EXAMPLE 4 — Analogy as Solution Proposal
**Register:** Design / technical

> "I don't like the idea of Notion not getting updated at least once a day. Maybe we
> can do like a GitHub where, as I finish stuff locally, I can say 'Sync to Notion',
> and then we can update Notion. How's that sound?"

**Techniques:**
1. Constraint first
2. "Maybe we can do like a [known reference]" — analogy IS the proposal
3. Concrete user action embedded in the analogy
4. Collaborative close: "How's that sound?"

---

## EXAMPLE 5 — Outcomes Before Method, Scope by Negation
**Register:** Technical / product

> "The goal isn't to see the full pipeline. I want to see the jobs that match and the
> progress of us getting through it. I just want to know, ultimately, when all the
> processes are done. I do think transparency is good, but I don't need a list of
> everything that's on the ATS feed. I just want to have some visibility into what's
> happening so I know when it's time."

**Techniques:**
1. Open by stating the wrong goal (negation of the inflated version)
2. "I just want to know, ultimately" — strips to essential
3. Concede the principle, reject the scope: "I do think X is good, but I don't need…"
4. Outcome expressed as a behavioral trigger: "so I know when it's time"

## EXAMPLE 6 — Personal Memory, No Preamble
**Register:** Personal / reflective

> "I remember a long time ago he was teaching me to drive in the E30. We were around
> Via Rancho Parkway. I was trying to get off on that exit but I panicked and he said
> something like, 'Okay you're panicking, let's calm down,' and helped me get over onto
> the next exit. That memory keeps flashing in my head lately. I just need to calm down
> and get off at the next exit."

**Techniques:**
1. Arrives with zero transitional framing — no "this reminds me of"
2. Hyper-specific detail: real car model, real street name
3. Honest imprecision: "something like" — does not reconstruct false precision
4. The memory becomes the metaphor — never explained, only enacted
5. Final line borrows the language of the memory as a personal instruction

**Note:** Do not replicate the pronoun shift in the original transcript ("he was like,
I don't remember exactly what *you* said"). That was a speech artifact. Keep pronouns
consistent in writing.

## EXAMPLE 7 — In-the-Moment Pivot + Self-Interested Reasoning
**Register:** Technical / product

> "No, okay so before you change any more code, I just want to plan and see what our
> implementation plan is going to be before we change anything. For sure I want to
> continue to web crawl sites like LinkedIn and Built In only because they have strict
> no-bot policies and I don't want to get my account banned. Can we do both? How would
> that look if that's what we did?"

**Techniques:**
1. "No, okay so" as the in-the-moment pivot signal
2. Self-interested reason stated plainly — no justification needed
3. Two-question close: possibility check, then method
4. "If that's what we did" — floats the option, does not declare it

## EXAMPLE 8 — Frustration That Produces a Decision
**Register:** Technical / product (same source passage as Example 11)

> "At this point I'm spending more time trying to get this to work than actually
> learning, so I want to strip out everything outside of having folders that we listen
> to and take a look at what's inside those folders and then get some help. That's what
> I want to focus on, right."

**Techniques:**
1. Opens with the actual cost — not the emotion, the cost
2. Frustration → decision, not frustration → complaint
3. "Strip out everything outside of X" — minimum viable scope
4. Long sentence + four-word closer: "That's what I want to focus on, right."
5. "Right" is an honest confirmation request, not decoration

## EXAMPLE 9 — Conceptual Framing via Spatial Metaphor
**Register:** Professional / career

> "I don't think product management is done in a vacuum. I like to think of spokes of
> a wheel where product as an organization is the center. Not to say I do think
> companies should be product-centric and customer-centric — but I think the spoke being
> in the center really communicates that it's very important that you're getting input
> and feedback from your customers as well as the rest of the business units. Product
> management, at the end of the day, is a communication job and distilling what you get
> out of that communication into value for your customers."

**Techniques:**
1. Negation opener: "not done in a vacuum"
2. Spatial metaphor as conceptual anchor
3. Immediate hedge on the metaphor to block a specific misreading — the wheel image
   could imply "product > customers"; he blocks that explicitly
4. "At the end of the day" = the core claim is about to land (professional register only)
5. Final sentence is the most polished — this is a rehearsed philosophy

---

## EXAMPLE 10 — Deadpan Two-Sentence Humor
**Register:** Personal / reflective

> "My triglycerides and cholesterol ballooned. I'll see you all in the next world."

**Techniques:**
1. Two sentences, zero setup
2. Factual register first sentence, theatrical register second
3. The gap between the two registers is the joke — never bridged
4. Zero explanation, zero winking

**Critical rule:** If a third sentence appears, the joke is dead.

---

## EXAMPLE 11 — Named Pivot, Provisional Future
**Register:** Technical / product (same source passage as Example 8)

> "OK, so I've been thinking about this tool quite a bit recently, and I have decided
> I want to go in a different direction. At first I was thinking what I wanted was a
> tool for non-technical people to use, but now I think I will dog food the logic first,
> and then, if later I feel like it's really good, I want to build it into its own app."

**Techniques:**
1. "I've been thinking about this quite a bit recently" — signals deliberate arrival, not impulse
2. Name the pivot before explaining it
3. "At first… but now" for changed-mind moments — no shame, no over-explanation
4. Technical vocabulary deployed casually without definition ("dog food")
5. Future phases held provisionally: "if later I feel like it's really good"
